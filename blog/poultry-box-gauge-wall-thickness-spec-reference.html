Poultry Box Spec Reference: Gauge, Wall Thickness, and Durability

Poultryboxes Editorial • October 2026

Reading a Poultry Box Spec Sheet

When you compare poultry boxes across suppliers, the spec sheet is where durability promises become measurable. Two boxes that look identical can differ by 30 percent in material thickness, and that difference shows up directly in service life, impact tolerance, and ultimately cost per trip. For procurement teams, learning to read gauge and wall-thickness specs is the fastest way to separate a box built for hundreds of cycles from one that cracks after a rough season.

Material specification is where a PP poultry box from Elipacko is defined before anything else — the resin grade and wall thickness determine how the box behaves under load, impact, and temperature over its full service life.

Wall Thickness and Structural Grade

Wall thickness is the headline number, usually expressed in millimeters for molded PP boxes. Thicker walls resist deflection under stacked load and absorb impact without cracking, but add weight and material cost. The right thickness balances durability against handling ergonomics and freight weight.

  • Light-duty — thinner walls for low-cycle or single-season handling
  • Standard-duty — mid-range thickness for routine multi-cycle transport
  • Heavy-duty — thick walls and reinforced ribs for high-impact, high-frequency loops

Ribbing matters as much as raw thickness. Well-placed structural ribs let a box achieve high stiffness without excess material, so compare rib design alongside the wall-thickness number rather than in isolation.

Impact Resistance and Cold Performance

Poultry handling is rough: boxes are dropped, stacked, and moved through chilled environments. Impact resistance is governed by resin grade and thickness together. Standard polypropylene can become brittle at low temperatures, so for cold-dock or refrigerated handling, specify an impact-modified or copolymer PP rated for the temperature range you operate in. Ask the supplier for a notched impact value and the minimum service temperature, not just a generic durability claim.

Load Ratings: Static vs Dynamic

A box rated to hold a given weight sitting still may carry far less in a vibrating trailer. Request both the static load rating (stacked and stationary) and the dynamic rating (in transit). For live-bird transport, factor in the animal weight plus the shock loads of loading and road vibration. A conservative spec with a safety margin prevents the collapsed-column losses that erase any savings from a thinner, cheaper box.

How Thickness Drives Cost Per Trip

Cost per trip is the metric that makes thickness decisions rational. A heavier, thicker box costs more up front but may deliver three or four times the cycles of a light-duty unit before failure. Divide the purchase price by the realistic number of trips and the thicker box often wins decisively. Build this calculation into your evaluation and standardize it across your handling fleet — the same logic applies to crates and other equipment in the broader Elipacko agriculture packaging range.

A Spec Comparison Checklist

  • Record wall thickness in millimeters and note rib reinforcement
  • Confirm resin grade and minimum service temperature
  • Request notched impact value for cold-handling applications
  • Capture both static and dynamic load ratings
  • Compute cost per trip using realistic cycle counts

Specs are not boilerplate — they are the contract for how long your boxes will survive real handling. Buyers who read gauge, wall thickness, and load ratings carefully, then convert them into cost per trip, consistently build fleets that outlast cheaper alternatives. Put the numbers side by side and let the thickness earn its price.
